IAudit of the Current Proposal

Before adding anything, the existing material has to be understood precisely. This Part states what each document in the repository actually contains, maps the coverage of the four reference papers against the proposal's claims, and records every defect that must be corrected before the proposal can be submitted or defended.

Headline finding

The proposal is substantively sound but generically framed. Its architecture, methodology and evaluation plan are largely correct and defensible. The problem is not that the idea is wrong — it is that almost every specific claim in it has already been published by one of the four papers sitting in the same folder. As written, a reviewer who reads the proposal and then reads Wang (2026) or Dashtaki et al. (2025) would conclude that the proposal contributes no new method. The fix is a repositioning of the contribution from architecture to scope, tiering, and an asset-class-split sentiment treatment — which the four papers collectively leave open. Part II specifies that repositioning.

The single most damaging sentence in the current proposal

Section 3 states: "No cross-asset contagion or systemic risk modeling exists, as cryptocurrencies are treated independently." This is the strongest and most defensible gap identified in the document, and it is the one the team should build the entire project around. It is also the gap that survives all four papers: Wang (2026) is BTC-only with macro context but no other crypto; Dashtaki et al. (2025) are BTC-only; Wang & Huang (2026) treat four assets independently with no cross-asset modelling; Kreuzer et al. (2026) is the only source with two assets and it observes contagion in a regression rather than modelling it.

1.4 Overstated novelty claim

Section 5 of the proposal closes with the following sentence, and it is the most dangerous paragraph in the document:

"Despite these advances, no existing study integrates blockchain fundamentals, high-frequency multi-source sentiment, macroeconomic indicators, and advanced forecasting techniques within a single unified framework."

This is factually false against the repository. Wang (2026) integrates technical indicators, macro variables and cross-asset ratios in a single framework. Dashtaki et al. (2025) integrate market indicators and sentiment in a single framework. The claim asserts a gap that two of the four papers in the same folder already fill. An examiner who asks "has anyone done this?" — which is the one question a capstone defence always asks — receives an answer the student cannot support.

The fix is not to soften the claim. It is to replace the claim with a true and more specific one. The true claim is narrower and considerably stronger, because it is defensible line by line:

Recommended replacement for the §5 closing paragraph

"Recent work has established that sentiment-augmented market models (Dashtaki et al., 2025), technical–macroeconomic fusion (Wang, 2026), crypto-specific sentiment weighting (Kreuzer et al., 2026) and multi-asset evaluation (Wang & Huang, 2026) each improve cryptocurrency forecasting. However, these lines of work remain separated: each treats the cryptocurrency universe either as a single asset to be predicted, or as a set of assets to be predicted independently, with sentiment treated as a uniform exogenous input. No study models the structure of the sentiment channel across the asset class — specifically, whether the sentiment-to-return relationship differs systematically by asset tier, whether sentiment spills over from large-cap to high-beta assets with a measurable lag, or whether the reliability of sentiment itself is conditional on the prevailing market regime. This research addresses these gaps by developing a tier-stratified, regime-aware, contagion-aware forecasting framework that treats sentiment as a structured, asset-dependent signal rather than a uniform feature vector."

1.6 Formatting and reference hygiene

  • Acronym table. Rebuild in full from Appendix F. Do not patch it in place — every row is suspect.
  • Two tables of contents. Delete the manual one; auto-generate.
  • Unnumbered body headings. "Introduction", "Project/Research Motivation" and "Problem Statement" appear as unnumbered headings separate from the numbered sections, producing three headings for the same content. Number consistently 1–11 and reference them in the TOC.
  • Figure and table numbering. The proposal has "Fig (1)" and "Fig2" with inconsistent spacing and no table captions at all. Adopt the standard in §7.6: "Figure 3.1 —" with a caption below, "Table 3.2 —" with a caption above.
  • References. Eighteen entries for a 17-page proposal is thin relative to the four papers in the repository, which cite 40+ each. Expand to 30–40 with the expanded reading list in Appendix G, ensuring every in-text claim about a gap is supported by a citation.
  • Page numbering and headers. The proposal uses "N | Page" footers of inconsistent format. Adopt a single footer: chapter-page on the left, short title on the right.
  • Language. The introduction is written in an explanatory register ("This means no single company or authority decides how it's managed") appropriate for a general audience, not a research proposal. Section 1 should be rewritten to define the research problem and its significance, not define what cryptocurrency is.

Summary of Part I

The proposal has a sound skeleton and an incomplete fill. Fourteen of the thirty recorded defects are Critical. None of them require new research to fix — they require a re-framing, a rebuild of the requirements and acronym artefacts, and the addition of the four components that are genuinely absent: hypotheses, a prediction-task definition, a baseline set, and a cross-asset contagion module. Part II specifies the re-framing; Parts III–VI specify the build that fills the gaps.
