IIRepositioning the Research

Part I established that the current framing is not defensible because its central novelty claim is contradicted by the literature in the same folder. This Part constructs a framing that is defensible, shows exactly how it differs from each of the four source papers, and specifies the hypotheses that will be tested.

2.1 The genuine white space

Reading the four papers together rather than separately reveals a pattern that none of them states about itself. Consider what each treats as fixed:

PaperWhat it treats as fixedWhat it therefore cannot ask
Dashtaki et al. (2025) The asset (BTC), and sentiment as a uniform 3-vector per day Whether the same sentiment vector carries different information for a large-cap than for a high-beta small-cap; whether it carries different information in a bull market than in a crash.
Wang (2026) The asset (BTC); regime inferred from price only Whether sentiment helps identify the regime rather than merely being conditioned on it; whether a model can be made to degrade gracefully instead of failing outright under regime shift.
Kreuzer et al. (2026) The sentiment→return mapping as a stable, asset-specific elasticity Whether that mapping varies by asset tier; whether the BTC→ETH spillover they observe can be operationalised as a predictive feature rather than a side remark.
Wang & Huang (2026) Assets as independent prediction targets; sentiment absent entirely Anything at all about how sentiment links assets to one another.

Table 2.1 — What each source holds constant. The white space lies in what all four hold constant.

Stated as a single sentence: every existing study treats the sentiment-to-return relationship as a fixed, asset-independent, regime-independent constant. This project treats it as a structured function of asset tier and market regime, and operationalises the cross-asset structure as a predictive feature.

Three defensible contributions

C1 — Tier-conditional sentiment elasticity. Estimate the sentiment-to-return elasticity separately by asset tier (large-cap, mid-cap, high-beta/new listing) and test whether it differs significantly. The hypothesis is that sentiment sensitivity scales with market depth and listing maturity: deep, long-established markets absorb sentiment into price slowly and efficiently, while thin, newly-listed, high-beta assets react sharply and with more noise.

C2 — Operationalised contagion. Convert the BTC→ETH sentiment spillover observed by Kreuzer et al. into a leading indicator: measure lead–lag structure across the asset universe and feed lagged large-cap sentiment as an explicit cross-asset feature into small-cap models. Test whether it improves out-of-sample accuracy for the receiving assets.

C3 — Regime-conditional model routing. Detect market regime from a combination of price-based and sentiment-based signals, then route to a regime-appropriate model (or apply regime-conditional calibration). Test whether sentiment adds more predictive value in high-volatility regimes than in trending ones.

Hypotheses

IDHypothesisTest & decision rule
H1The crypto-density-weighted sentiment measure (CDW) exhibits a significantly stronger contemporaneous association with forward returns than the unweighted Loughran–McDonald measure.Nested regression; β difference tested by t-statistic. Reject H0 at α = 0.05.
H2The sentiment elasticity |βsentiment| decreases monotonically as asset tier moves from high-beta/newly-listed to large-cap established.Test of ordered interaction coefficients across the three tiers; Jonckheere–Terpstra trend test. Reject H0 if the ordering is not monotone at α = 0.05.
H3Lagged large-cap (BTC) sentiment adds statistically significant out-of-sample predictive power for non-BTC assets over and above their own sentiment and own price features.Nested model comparison (F2 vs F3) with Diebold–Mariano test on loss differentials; reject H0 if DM p < 0.05 in the majority of receiving assets.
H4The predictive contribution of sentiment is larger in high-volatility and bear regimes than in trending/bull regimes.Regime-conditional metric decomposition with interaction terms; test of the sentiment×regime interaction coefficient. Reject H0 at α = 0.05.

Table 2.3 — Hypotheses with explicit nulls and decision rules. Note the asymmetry in H2: this is the hypothesis most likely to be refuted, and a refutation that is well-supported is a publishable finding, not a failure.

Note on H2

If sentiment turns out to matter more for large-caps, H2 is refuted. That is an acceptable and interesting outcome, and it should be written up as such. A thesis that reports a clean refutation of a plausible hypothesis, supported by a well-powered test across a stratified panel, is stronger work than one that reports a marginal confirmation of the expected direction. Build the experiment so that either outcome is reportable — that means the tiers must be large enough and the test must have adequate power, which is why Part VI §6.2 specifies minimum tier sample sizes.

2.4 Contribution statement

Use this verbatim in §5 of the proposal and in §1.4 of the thesis.

This research makes four contributions.

(1) A crypto-density-weighted, news-sourced sentiment measure operationalised for a multi-tier asset panel. Extending the dictionary-plus-density-weighting logic of Kreuzer et al. (2026) to a panel spanning large-cap, mid-cap and high-beta assets, and establishing the measure's reliability against a generic financial-dictionary baseline across tiers.

(2) Evidence on tier-conditional sentiment elasticity. The first systematic test of whether the sentiment-to-return relationship is structurally uniform across the cryptocurrency asset class. We hypothesise it is not, and that the gradient follows market depth and listing maturity.

(3) An operationalised cross-asset contagion framework. Converting the BTC→ETH sentiment spillover reported by Kreuzer et al. (2026) into a predictive feature by measuring lead–lag structure across the panel and testing whether lagged large-cap sentiment adds out-of-sample value for receiving assets. We further examine whether contagion intensity is itself tier-graded — that is, whether spillover attenuates with the distance between tiers.

(4) A regime-conditional routing and evaluation protocol. A framework that detects regime from combined price and sentiment signals, routes to regime-appropriate models, and — critically — reports performance decomposed by regime and by asset tier, rather than as a single aggregate number. This directly addresses the validation weakness identified by Wang (2026), whose own findings show that single-split aggregate metrics obscure substantial regime-dependent degradation.

Explicitly not claimed: a new neural architecture; a new sentiment model; a new validation method; a new trading strategy. Every such component is drawn from existing published work and credited accordingly.

2.6 How to use each of the four papers

These are the four papers' correct role in your literature review. Misusing them — citing them as if they supported your method rather than as the competition — is the most common way capstone proposals fail their own literature review.

You are not proposing a new fusion architecture. You adopt their fusion as a baseline and show that its benefit is not uniform across asset tiers and regimes. Implement HSIF-style cross-attention as one model in your ladder, restricted to the assets you can support. Report its performance decomposed by tier and regime — that decomposition is your finding, and it requires no new architecture.

The one-sentence positioning statement

Your work is not "a better model for crypto prediction" — it is "a study of how the structure of the sentiment channel differs across the cryptocurrency asset class, and whether that structure is exploitable." The models are the instrument; the asset-class structure of sentiment is the object of study. Write the thesis with that object in mind and every experimental choice follows naturally: you stratify by tier because the object is tier-dependent, you test lead–lag because the object has cross-sectional structure, you decompose by regime because the object's strength varies with conditions, and you validate economically because a sentiment channel that does not survive costs is not a channel.
